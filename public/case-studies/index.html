Case Studies.

Real repositories onboarded to slop-mop Maintenance Mode with sm refit, written up start to finish. Each one separates what was genuinely fixed from what was merely baselined, and each ends with a ledger of the bugs the run found in slop-mop itself.

Why · refit, then maintenance

slop-mop runs in two modes that do very different jobs. Knowing which one a case study is about is half of reading it honestly.

Refit · one-time Onboarding. The single goal is a seaworthy ship: a repo with no known issues and a committed baseline. You run it once, and these case studies are about that run.
Maintenance · every watch sm swab → sm scour → sm buff. The steady state, and where the lion’s share of the real code-quality work gets exercised. The buff cycle — turning CI results and review feedback into the next fix — is the throughput multiplier that makes the whole thing pay off.

Why bother with refit at all? Because you cannot swab and scour mechanically every watch and trust the result unless you start from a known baseline. Refit draws that line. After it, everything maintenance flags is new slop — from the change in front of you — not years of accumulated legacy noise. That line is what makes the daily loop fast and worth trusting.

The ideal refit is as frictionless as possible. Fixing obvious slop is not friction — auto-fix handles that for free. The friction worth naming is slop-mop’s own: this is a young tool with limited support, and every run below surfaced real rough edges in the tool itself. We log those as barnacles rather than hide them, and each case study ends with its own.

And “refit didn’t fix much” cuts both ways. It can mean the repo already had strong practices — or that slop-mop disabled gates and lowered thresholds just to go green, which is exactly what we don’t want. A green board does not distinguish the two, so every study below carries a table splitting what was genuinely fixed from what was suppressed, baselined, or scoped out. Read that table before the scoreboard.

The runs

Two so far, deliberately at opposite ends of the size range — because the interesting question is not whether a board goes green, but what refit is actually doing to get there.

  • rulebook-ai, cold

    ~8.5k lines · Python · picked from the wild

    Start
    8 gates failing
    Finish
    A+ · 0 findings
    Suppressions
    9
    Our bugs found
    10

    A repo that had never heard of us, with no coordination and nothing pre-cleaned. At this size refit genuinely cleans: two latent crashes, a CI pipeline that had been failing since GitHub retired upload-artifact@v3 in September 2025, a dependency pin that will not build, and a pytest config that stopped the suite from booting.

    Mostly real fixes. Coverage was the only thing baselined, strict typing was satisfied rather than downgraded — and the worst entry in its ledger is ours: a security fix that deleted a supported feature and broke three of their tests.

    A massive developer assistant platform, and the honest word for most of this one is baselining. Coverage was frozen at the existing 49%, two strict-typing gates were downgraded, and the front-end gate suite was scoped out. A small set of genuine fixes came with it — least-privilege Actions permissions, a corrected sandbox test, and duplicate pagination titles extracted across 11 routers.

    Refit bought a clean starting line, not a rewrite of history. On a codebase this size that is the honest ceiling — and the run also had us reformatting 94 applied database migrations, which it should never have touched.

What the two case studies say together

On a 322k-line legacy codebase, refit bought a starting line and little else — the honest word for most of it was baselining. On an 8.5k-line one, the same command found two crashes, a year-dead CI pipeline, an uninstallable dependency pin, and a test suite that could not boot. Size is the difference we noticed, and the intuition it suggests — that the volume of legacy findings is what forces baselining — is a reasonable one. But two repositories is not a study. They also differ in age, dependency health, CI state, and how much type checking they had ever seen, and any of those could be doing the work. Treat it as a hypothesis worth more data points, not a finding.

The other thing both runs share: the tool broke something each time, and each time it went in the ledger. Ten barnacles from the rulebook-ai run alone, plus two from OpenHands — all filed against ourselves, all fixed. If a quality tool cannot be honest about its own defects, it has no standing to grade yours.

Lessons learned: What refit can do depends heavily on what it is pointed at. On a large legacy codebase it is about identifying structural risks, locking in a baseline, and scoping rules so future PRs are validated without legacy noise. On a smaller one it can genuinely clean. Both are useful outcomes; they are just not the same outcome, and a green board on its own will not tell you which one you got.

It is also about being honest when the tool gets it wrong. Across these two runs, twelve findings were slop-mop's own — two on OpenHands (#263, #264) and ten on rulebook-ai (#331, #332), including one case where our own fix was worse than the bug it replaced. That is the same friction-reporting loop we ask every user to run. We would rather show you the warts and the fixes than a flawless screenshot.
